## From the Abacus to the First Commercial Computer {#from-the-abacus-to-the-first-commercial-computer}

The history of computers runs from simple counting tools, through mechanical and electromechanical calculating machines, to electronic computers and finally to the first computers sold commercially.

### 1. Abacus

- The **abacus** is the earliest known calculating device.
- It has beads that slide on rods or wires inside a frame. By moving the beads, a person can perform addition and subtraction quickly.
- The earliest known abacus was used in **Mesopotamia (Sumer) between about 2700 and 2300 BC**. The Chinese abacus, called the **suanpan**, is documented from the 2nd century BC and is the best-known form. (Guidebooks give different dates, such as 3000 BC or 4000 BC, because the exact date is not known.)

### 2. Napier's Bones (1617)

- Invented by the Scottish mathematician **John Napier**, and published in **1617** in his book *Rabdologiae*.
- It is a set of numbered rods (made of ivory).
- It is used for **multiplication and division**: multiplication is reduced to additions, and division to subtractions.

### 3. Pascaline (1642)

- Invented by the French mathematician **Blaise Pascal** in **1642**, when he was about 18 years old.
- He built it to help his father, a tax supervisor, with long arithmetic calculations.
- It was a **mechanical calculator** with gears and wheels. It could **add directly**; subtraction was done by the nines' complement method, and multiplication and division by repeated addition or subtraction.
- Pascal sold about twenty of these machines. It is known as the **first mechanical calculator** (also called *Pascal's calculator*).

### 4. Stepped Reckoner / Leibniz Calculator (1673)

- Invented by the German mathematician **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz**. He presented a model to the Royal Society of London in **1673**.
- It improved on Pascal's machine. It was the **first calculator that could perform all four basic arithmetic operations**: addition, subtraction, multiplication and division.
- Its key part was a **stepped drum**, now called the *Leibniz wheel*.

### 5. Jacquard Loom (1804)

- Invented by **Joseph Marie Jacquard**, who patented it in **1804**.
- It was a weaving loom controlled by a chain of **punched cards**, which automatically produced complex woven patterns.
- Its idea of controlling a machine with punched cards was used by Charles Babbage for his Analytical Engine and by Herman Hollerith for his tabulating machine, so it is an important forerunner of computer programming and data input.

### 6. Difference Engine (1822)

- Designed by the English mathematician **Charles Babbage**, who announced it in **1822**.
- It was an **automatic mechanical calculator** designed to compute tables of polynomial values using the *method of finite differences*.
- It was never completed in Babbage's lifetime: the British government stopped funding it in 1842.

### 7. Analytical Engine (1837)

- Designed by **Charles Babbage**, who first described it in **1837**.
- It was the **first design for a general-purpose, programmable mechanical computer**. Its parts match the parts of a modern computer:

| Part of the Analytical Engine | Function | Modern equivalent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Store | Held numbers (memory for 1,000 numbers) | Memory |
| Mill | Performed arithmetic operations | Arithmetic Logic Unit (CPU) |
| Punched cards | Gave instructions and data to the machine | Input |
| Printer | Printed the results | Output |

- It could also take decisions (conditional branching) and repeat steps (loops).
- It was **never built** in Babbage's lifetime.
- Because his design contained all the basic parts of a modern computer, **Charles Babbage is called the "Father of the Computer"**.

#### Ada Lovelace: the first computer programmer

- **Ada Lovelace** worked with Babbage. In **1843** she published notes on the Analytical Engine that included a method (algorithm) for calculating Bernoulli numbers on the machine.
- This is widely considered the **first computer program**, so Ada Lovelace is known as the **first computer programmer**.

### 8. Tabulating Machine (1890)

- Invented by **Herman Hollerith**.
- It was an **electromechanical machine** that read data recorded as holes in **punched cards** and counted (tabulated) it automatically.
- It was used for the **1890 United States census**. Processing the census took six years, compared with eight years for the 1880 census.
- Hollerith founded the **Tabulating Machine Company in 1896**. It was merged into the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company (CTR) in 1911, which was renamed **International Business Machines (IBM) in 1924**.

### 9. Differential Analyzer (1931)

- Built by **Vannevar Bush** and Harold Hazen at the **Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT)** between **1928 and 1931**.
- It was a **mechanical analog computer** used to solve **differential equations**.

### 10. Turing Machine (1936)

- Described by the mathematician **Alan Turing** in **1936**.
- It is not a physical machine but a **theoretical (mathematical) model of computation**: an abstract machine that reads and writes symbols on a tape according to a set of rules.
- It showed that a single machine can carry out any algorithm, and it is the basis of the theory of modern computers.

### 11. Z3 (1941)

- Built by **Konrad Zuse** in Berlin, Germany, completed in **1941**.
- It was an **electromechanical** computer built with about 2,600 relays.
- It is regarded as the **world's first working programmable, fully automatic digital computer**.

### 12. Atanasoff–Berry Computer (ABC) (1942)

- Built by **John Vincent Atanasoff** and his graduate student **Clifford Berry** at Iowa State College (USA). It was conceived in 1937 and successfully tested in **1942**.
- It used more than 300 **vacuum tubes** and **binary** arithmetic, and was designed only to solve systems of linear equations.
- It was **not programmable**.
- In 1973 a US court (Honeywell v. Sperry Rand) ruled that ENIAC's designers had not themselves first invented the automatic electronic digital computer, but had derived it from Atanasoff's work. For this reason, the ABC is often called the **first automatic electronic digital computer**, although historians still debate this, because it was not programmable.

### 13. Harvard Mark I (1944)

- Designed by **Howard Aiken** of Harvard University and built by **IBM**. Its official name was the **IBM Automatic Sequence Controlled Calculator (ASCC)**.
- It was completed and presented to Harvard University in **1944**.
- It was a very large **electromechanical** computer (about 51 feet long, with about 765,000 parts) and was programmed with **punched paper tape**.

### 14. ENIAC (1945)

- **ENIAC** stands for **Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer**.
- Designed by **John Mauchly** and **J. Presper Eckert** at the Moore School of Electrical Engineering, **University of Pennsylvania**.
- Completed in **1945** and announced to the public in **February 1946**.
- It used about **18,000 vacuum tubes**, weighed more than 27 tonnes and consumed about 150 kW of electric power.
- It was the **first programmable, electronic, general-purpose digital computer**.
- It did not store its program in memory: it was programmed by wiring plugboards and setting switches.

### 15. Stored-Program Computers (1945–1948)

- In **June 1945**, **John von Neumann** wrote the *First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC*. It described the **stored-program concept**: both the program (instructions) and the data are stored in the same memory of the computer. This design is called the **von Neumann architecture**.
- The **Manchester Baby** (Small-Scale Experimental Machine), built at the University of Manchester by Frederic C. Williams, Tom Kilburn and Geoff Tootill, ran its first program on **21 June 1948**. It was the **first electronic stored-program computer**.

### 16. UNIVAC I: the First Commercial Computer (1951)

- **UNIVAC I** stands for **Universal Automatic Computer I**.
- Designed by **J. Presper Eckert** and **John Mauchly**, the designers of ENIAC, and built by their company, which was taken over by **Remington Rand**.
- The first UNIVAC I was accepted by the **United States Census Bureau** on **31 March 1951**.
- It was the first general-purpose electronic digital computer designed for **business and administrative use** produced in the United States, so it is known as the **first commercial computer**.
- It used about 6,103 vacuum tubes, and 46 systems were built.
- In 1952 it became famous for correctly predicting **Dwight D. Eisenhower's** victory in the US presidential election.

> **Note:** UNIVAC I is the answer usually expected for "first commercial computer". Strictly, the British **Ferranti Mark 1** was delivered to the University of Manchester a little earlier, in **February 1951**, and is described as the world's first commercially available general-purpose electronic computer.
