## First to Fifth Generation {#first-to-fifth-generation}

### What is a Computer Generation?

A **computer generation** is a stage in the development of computers that is marked by a major change in the basic technology (hardware) used to build them. Each new generation made computers, compared with the one before:

1. Smaller
2. Faster
3. Cheaper
4. More reliable
5. More energy-efficient

### Five Generations of Computers

The development of computers is divided into **five generations**:

| Generation | Main technology |
| --- | --- |
| First | Vacuum tubes |
| Second | Transistors |
| Third | Integrated circuits (ICs) |
| Fourth | Microprocessors (LSI and VLSI) |
| Fifth | Artificial intelligence (ULSI, parallel processing) |

> **Note on periods:** The years of each generation are approximate, and different books give slightly different years. The periods below follow TutorialsPoint (TpointTech gives the same periods, except that it starts the third generation in 1964); GeeksforGeeks gives them by decade (1940s–1950s, 1950s–1960s, 1960s–1970s, 1970s–present, present and future).

#### 1. First Generation (1940–1956): Vacuum Tubes

**Technology:** The first-generation computers used **vacuum tubes** (also called thermionic valves) for their circuits.

**Characteristics:**

1. Used vacuum tubes as the main electronic component.
2. **Very large in size**: a single computer filled a whole room.
3. **Consumed a lot of electricity** and **produced a lot of heat**.
4. Not very reliable: vacuum tubes failed often.
5. **Very expensive** to build and maintain.
6. Used **magnetic drums** for memory.
7. Used **punched cards and paper tape** for input and output.
8. Programmed in **machine language** (binary 0s and 1s); assembly language appeared later.
9. Had no operating system.

**Examples:**

1. ENIAC
2. EDVAC
3. UNIVAC I
4. IBM 650
5. IBM 701

#### 2. Second Generation (1956–1963): Transistors

**Technology:** The second-generation computers used **transistors** in place of vacuum tubes.

**Characteristics:**

1. Used transistors, which are much **smaller, faster, more reliable** and use **less power** than vacuum tubes.
2. **Smaller** in size than first-generation computers.
3. Produced **less heat** and consumed **less electricity**.
4. **Faster** in computation.
5. Used **magnetic core memory** as the main memory.
6. Used **punched cards and magnetic tape** for input and output.
7. Programmed in **assembly language** and the first **high-level languages**: FORTRAN (1957) and COBOL (1959).
8. Used in business, science and government.

**Examples:**

1. IBM 1401
2. IBM 7090
3. UNIVAC 1107
4. CDC 3600

#### 3. Third Generation (1963–1971): Integrated Circuits

**Technology:** The third-generation computers used **integrated circuits (ICs)**. An IC (also called a *chip*) places many transistors and other components on a single small piece of silicon. The first working IC was demonstrated by **Jack Kilby** (Texas Instruments) in **1958**, and **Robert Noyce** (Fairchild Semiconductor) made the first monolithic IC in **1959**.

**Characteristics:**

1. Used ICs in place of individual transistors.
2. **Smaller, faster and more reliable** than second-generation computers.
3. Consumed **less power**, produced **less heat**, and had a **lower maintenance cost**.
4. **Keyboards and monitors** were used for input and output, along with magnetic tape and printers.
5. Used **operating systems** that could run several programs at the same time.
6. Programmed in **high-level languages** such as FORTRAN, COBOL and BASIC.
7. **Minicomputers** appeared, and computers were widely used commercially.

**Examples:**

1. IBM System/360 (1964)
2. IBM 370
3. PDP-11
4. UNIVAC 1108

#### 4. Fourth Generation (1972–2010): Microprocessors

**Technology:** The fourth-generation computers used **microprocessors**. A microprocessor is a single chip that contains the complete central processing unit (CPU). It is made with **large-scale integration (LSI)** and **very-large-scale integration (VLSI)**. The first commercial microprocessor was the **Intel 4004**, released in **1971** (4-bit, 2,300 transistors).

**Characteristics:**

1. Used microprocessors built with LSI and VLSI technology.
2. **Very small** and **portable**; computers could be placed on a desk or carried.
3. **Very low power consumption** and **affordable** price.
4. Used **semiconductor memory** (RAM and ROM).
5. Used the keyboard, pointing devices (mouse), optical scanning, monitor and printer for input and output.
6. **Personal computers (microcomputers)** became available to the general public.
7. **Computer networks** developed, and features such as multiprocessing, time-sharing and virtual memory were introduced.

**Examples:**

1. IBM PC
2. Apple II
3. Apple Macintosh
4. Altair 8800

#### 5. Fifth Generation (2010–Present): Artificial Intelligence

**Technology:** The fifth generation is based on **artificial intelligence (AI)**. It uses:

1. **Ultra-large-scale integration (ULSI)**
2. **Parallel processing**
3. Quantum computation
4. Nanotechnology

**Characteristics:**

1. Aim to make computers think and behave more like human beings (artificial intelligence).
2. **Very high speed**, and can perform many tasks at the same time (parallel processing).
3. **Smallest size** and fully **portable**.
4. **Very low power consumption**.
5. Can understand **natural (human) language**, and accept input by **touch, pen and speech** as well as keyboard and mouse.
6. Full artificial intelligence has not yet been achieved; development is still continuing.

**Examples:**

1. Laptops
2. Tablets
3. Smartphones
4. AI systems and assistants such as IBM Watson and Apple's Siri

> **Note:** Japan's Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI) started the **Fifth Generation Computer Systems (FGCS)** project in **1982**. It aimed to build parallel computers for artificial intelligence using logic programming. For this reason, some books start the fifth generation in the 1980s.

### Scale of Integration

The terms used for the third, fourth and fifth generations describe how many transistors are placed on one chip:

| Term | Full form | Transistors per chip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SSI | Small-Scale Integration | 1 to 10 |
| MSI | Medium-Scale Integration | 10 to 500 |
| LSI | Large-Scale Integration | 500 to 20,000 |
| VLSI | Very-Large-Scale Integration | 20,000 to 1,000,000 |
| ULSI | Ultra-Large-Scale Integration | More than 1,000,000 |

### Comparison of the Five Generations

| Basis | First | Second | Third | Fourth | Fifth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Period (approx.) | 1940–1956 | 1956–1963 | 1963–1971 | 1972–2010 | 2010–present |
| Main technology | Vacuum tubes | Transistors | Integrated circuits | Microprocessors (LSI, VLSI) | AI, ULSI, parallel processing |
| Size | Room-sized | Smaller | Smaller still | Desktop and portable | Smallest, portable |
| Power and heat | Very high | Less | Less still | Very low | Very low |
| Input/output | Punched cards, paper tape | Punched cards, magnetic tape | Keyboard, monitor | Keyboard, mouse, monitor | Touch, speech, keyboard |
| Language | Machine language | Assembly, FORTRAN, COBOL | High-level (BASIC and others) | High-level | Natural language |
| Examples | ENIAC, UNIVAC I | IBM 1401, IBM 7090 | IBM System/360 | IBM PC, Apple II | Laptops, smartphones |
